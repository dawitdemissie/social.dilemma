Question 1 of 10

Crowds vs. professional fact-checkers

In January 2025, Meta announced it would end its third-party fact-checking program in the United States and move to a “Community Notes” system like the one on X. Ordinary users write notes on misleading posts, and a note appears only when users who usually disagree with each other rate it helpful.

What the public is saying

  • Supporters say crowds are faster, can cover far more posts, and are trusted more because no single group decides what’s true.
  • Critics say notes often arrive after a false post has already gone viral, many accurate notes are never shown, and volunteers may lack expertise.
Collective intelligence (crowds of users) can do a better job than professional fact-checkers at stopping misinformation.

Question 2 of 10

Airbnb and the housing crunch

New York City began enforcing Local Law 18 in September 2023. Hosts must register, and for stays under 30 days the host generally has to live in the home, which cut the number of listings sharply. In 2024, Barcelona announced a plan to phase out all tourist-apartment licenses by 2028. Residents in several tourist cities have protested over rising rents.

What the public is saying

  • Hosts and travelers say short-term rentals give families extra income, offer cheaper and more local stays, and bring tourist spending to more neighborhoods.
  • Residents and housing advocates say whole apartments turned into year-round tourist rentals take homes off the market, push up rents and change neighborhoods.
Cities should strictly limit short-term rentals like Airbnb.

Question 3 of 10

Is the gig economy really “sharing”?

Uber and Lyft treat drivers as independent contractors, not employees. In 2020, California voters approved Proposition 22 with about 58% of the vote. It kept app-based drivers as contractors but added some benefits, such as a minimum earnings guarantee for time spent on trips and a health care stipend for some drivers.

What the public is saying

  • Many drivers value being able to choose when, where and how much they work.
  • Labor groups argue drivers carry the costs and risks (car, fuel, insurance, no sick pay) while the company sets prices and controls the work.
Ride-hailing drivers should be treated as employees, not independent contractors.
Where do you stand?

Question 4 of 10

Should algorithms fix polarization?

HW #2 suggested platforms could “use algorithms to show diverse viewpoints.” In 2023, researchers working with Meta published large studies of Facebook and Instagram during the 2020 U.S. election. Switching users to a chronological feed changed what they saw, but did not measurably change their political attitudes during the study.

What the public is saying

  • Some people want platforms to actively show opposing views and stop rewarding outrage.
  • Others say it’s not a platform’s job to decide what counts as “balanced,” and that polarization has deeper causes than feeds, such as cable news, geography and party politics.
Social media platforms are responsible for political polarization and should change their algorithms to fix it.
Where do you stand?

Question 5 of 10

Volunteers create it, companies profit

Reddit depends on unpaid volunteer moderators. In June 2023, thousands of communities went dark to protest new fees that forced many third-party apps to shut down. In 2024, Reddit went public and was reported to have signed a deal letting Google use its content for AI training, worth about $60 million a year.

What the public is saying

  • Many users argue that a company profiting from volunteer work owes the volunteers something.
  • Others say volunteers join freely, get a free platform in return, and that paying them could change why they contribute.
When a company profits from peer-produced content, the volunteers who created it deserve a share.

Question 6 of 10

Will AI eat the communities it learns from?

Stack Overflow is a peer-produced Q&A site for programmers. Its number of new questions dropped sharply after ChatGPT launched in late 2022. In 2024, it announced a partnership allowing OpenAI to use its content. Some users protested by deleting or changing their answers, and some of those accounts were suspended.

What the public is saying

  • Some say AI makes community knowledge easier to use, which helps everyone.
  • Others say if people ask AI instead of the community, fewer new answers get written, and future AI will have less fresh human knowledge to learn from.
AI tools that learn from peer-produced communities will eventually destroy those communities.

Question 7 of 10

Can officials block their critics?

HW #2 described governments using social media for real-time engagement. In Lindke v. Freed (2024), the U.S. Supreme Court said an official’s posts count as government action only if the official had authority to speak for the government on that matter and was using it. In that case, blocking someone may violate the First Amendment.

What the public is saying

  • Many say an elected official’s page is a public forum, and blocking critics silences voters.
  • Others say officials are also private people, and they need a way to stop harassment, threats and spam.
Elected officials should never be allowed to block citizens on social media.

Question 8 of 10

Banning an app for national security

In April 2024, Congress passed, with support from both parties, a law requiring TikTok’s China-based parent company, ByteDance, to sell its U.S. operations or face a ban. In January 2025, the U.S. Supreme Court upheld the law. Since then, how and when it is enforced has been the subject of ongoing political negotiation, so check the latest news.

What the public is saying

  • Supporters of the law point to concerns about data on U.S. users and possible foreign influence over what users see.
  • Opponents point to free expression for millions of users, creators’ and small businesses’ income, and the precedent of the government banning a communication platform.
Governments should be able to ban a popular social media app over national security concerns.

Question 9 of 10

Should under-16s be kept off social media?

In 2023, the U.S. Surgeon General issued an advisory warning that social media can pose risks to young people’s mental health, while noting that it also has benefits and that more research is needed. In November 2024, Australia passed a law requiring major platforms to stop children under 16 from having accounts, taking effect in December 2025.

What the public is saying

  • Many parents support age limits, saying platforms are designed to be addictive and expose teens to bullying and harmful content.
  • Critics say bans are hard to enforce, require age checks that threaten everyone’s privacy, and cut off teens who find support online, especially those who feel isolated.
Social media should be banned for everyone under 16.

Question 10 of 10

Does hashtag activism create real change?

Hashtags group conversations by topic (HW #2, Q5 and Q11). #MeToo spread worldwide in 2017 and led to public reckonings in many industries. The 2014 #IceBucketChallenge raised over $100 million for ALS research. Critics use the word “slacktivism” for online support that costs little effort and changes little.

What the public is saying

  • Supporters say hashtags give a voice to people traditional media ignore, and that they raise awareness and money fast.
  • Skeptics say liking or reposting can make people feel they’ve helped when nothing changes, and that hashtags can spread misinformation or pile-ons.
Hashtag activism creates real change.
